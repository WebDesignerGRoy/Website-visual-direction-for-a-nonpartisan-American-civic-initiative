    What Americans actually agree on

    Inside the national study behind one sentence — who was asked, what they said, and why the consensus holds in every group we measured.

    By the Research Team8 min readNational study conducted through Verasight

    Every conversation about American politics begins with the same word: divided. We are told the country has split into two tribes with no common ground — not even the ground under their feet. Our research set out to test whether that is true of one specific question: whether the American people have a right to honest government service.

    The answer is the most unanimous finding we have seen on any question of public life. 95.5 percent of Americans agree with that principle. Not a majority. Not a coalition. Essentially everyone — Republicans, Democrats, independents, and the growing share of voters who claim no party at all.

    “The American people have a right to honest government service.”

    The statement — signed by 48,000+ Americans

    Agreement with a principle is one thing; willingness to act on it is another. So we asked the question the way a ballot would: should the American people have a right to honest government service — yes or no? 73 percent said they would vote yes. Another 19 percent were undecided, which leaves just 8 percent opposed.

    About this study

    Conducted through Verasight, a national survey research firm, using a nationally representative sample of U.S. adults. Respondents were asked two questions: whether they agreed with the principle that “The American people have a right to honest government service,” and how they would vote on establishing that right. Results were weighted to census benchmarks for age, gender, race and ethnicity, education, and region. Full crosstabs and question wording are available on request.

    What does a right to honest government service mean in practice? It means a veteran’s benefits claim cannot disappear into a drawer. It means a small town’s permit cannot wait on a favor. It means the answer to “who do I call?” cannot be nobody. Honest service is not a partisan aspiration — it is the everyday promise every public agency makes, and too many keep only sometimes.

    Agreement this broad is a foundation, and foundations get built on. The next step is yours: read the statement, and if it says what you believe, sign it. One sentence. 95.5 percent of your neighbors already agree with it.

    Design Notes

    Direction

    Civic modernism with American archival warmth — the visual register of an institution citizens are still building: the national museum, the university press, the great public report. Paper and ink, gold hairlines, thick-and-thin newspaper rules, documentary photography, and one sentence set as if it matters.

    The neutrality test: cover the word “nonpartisan” and nothing on the page changes its meaning. The same courthouse, the same ledger, the same crosstabs showing Republicans at 94.8% and Democrats at 96.4%. Red, white, and blue appear only as deep historical tones — navy, cream, oxblood. No campaign red or blue, no yard-sign type, no stars, no eagles, no faux parchment, no gradients or glass.
